---
title: "Why is the letter “Я” a symbol of progress?"
---

Think of the Greeks, the Romans and similar dudes in rags. They wrote with whatever was lying on the ground: stones, twigs... And the Greek alphabet is built so that all the glyphs — the letters — are written from left to right.

Why?

Nobody shipped ctrl+Z to the stone slab. Every stroke has to be right the first time, and instead of an Apple Pencil you have a chisel and a little hammer.

The physical limits of writing. We write from left to right, so each letter can start where the previous one ends (allowing for letter spacing). How do you measure out the room for a letter that is written from right to left if you don’t know how much space it will take? Measure too much and you get an extra gap, measure too little and it won’t fit.

The Greek alphabet is ideal for those who write from left to right: it was designed from the start for setting large sheets of text with minimal mistakes and costs, in times when the price of a spelling, punctuation or aesthetic error was high.

The letter “Я” doesn’t fit into this system. Run an experiment: in your head, or better, on a sheet of paper. Start writing the letters “М”, “В”, “К”. Each letter starts on the left, and the next one joins the previous one nicely. If we need mathematical precision, we can build a grid, quickly check the width of the spacing and standardise the setting.

And what if you write the letter “Я” in this ideal system? Say you’ve measured the spacing. Then what? How far do you step back? By eye? And do I trust my eye enough to measure the perfect distance for a letter?

Letters aren’t just a set of sticks. They are a source of information and a way to pass it on, a purely practical thing. So a new system is needed. First, with a minimal chance of error — to make it easy to use. Second, built on the simple technologies of its time — to make it easy to reproduce.

And once such a system took shape, humanity began to scale fast. Stones and twigs gave way to quills, paper… We stopped depending on the tool we wrote with. And this is where the familiar import substitution enters the story.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

In the 18th century Peter the Great approved the form of the letter “Я” we know today, the one written from right to left. By then stone slabs, birch bark and clay tablets were long forgotten, and quills had become the new normal. For them, moving in any direction is no problem.

You can scribble the whole alphabet with no limits on how the hand moves. That’s what the Chinese did, for example: think of the characters — their writing system assumed from the start that the hand moves freely through space, and the art of calligraphy even grew out of that.

If humanity hadn’t chosen the path of globalisation and had stayed Greeks in rags, we would never have thought of flipping the “R” and creating the “Я”.

<div class="ids__space M"></div>

The fact that we can write the letter “Я” has become a symbol of progress for me, one that pushes me to be more curious about everything new and to look at the old like an innovator. What if it has already become a vestige?
